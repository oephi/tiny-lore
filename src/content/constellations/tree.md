The oldest tree in the forest had been alive so long it had forgotten its own name. It had watched rivers change their minds about which way to flow. It had seen mountains grow tired and sit down.

Its roots went so deep they touched the dreams of the earth itself — slow, warm dreams about stone and water and the patient work of growing.

Every creature in the forest had carved something into its bark at one time or another. Names, dates, tiny drawings of acorns. The tree didn't mind. It considered these its tattoos, and was rather proud of them.

On autumn evenings, when its leaves turned gold and let go one by one, the tree would tell its stories. Not in words — trees don't use words — but in the way its branches creaked, and the pattern its shadows made, and the particular rustle of each falling leaf.

If you sat very still and listened with your whole self, you could hear them: tales of every bird that had nested in its crown, every storm it had weathered, every spring morning it had watched the world wake up again.

The tree had no ending to its story. It just kept growing, one ring at a time, adding new chapters with every passing year.
